Where your compostable cup actually goes

12 June 2026

A customer sent us a photo of their own signage last month. It said, above the bin: “All our cups are compostable — put them in the green bin!” They’re in Hamilton. Hamilton does not accept compostable foodservice packaging in the green bin. The sign was wrong, we had sold them the cups, and nobody had told them.

So, plainly.

What the certification means

Our Hearth cups and lids and the Fieldpress sheets are BPI certified compostable. That certification tests whether the material breaks down under the conditions of an industrial composting facility: sustained heat, managed moisture, mechanical turning, a defined time window. It is a real standard and the cups genuinely pass it.

What it does not tell you is whether such a facility will ever receive your cup.

What municipalities actually do

Most Ontario green bin programs, Hamilton and Toronto included, currently exclude compostable foodservice packaging. Not because it doesn’t break down — because their sorting equipment can’t reliably tell a certified compostable cup from a plastic-lined one, and the cost of getting that wrong across a whole load is higher than the benefit of getting it right. Some smaller programs do accept it. A few accept sheets and liners but not cups. It varies, and it changes.

The honest answer for most of our Ontario and Quebec accounts is that a certified compostable cup put in the green bin gets pulled at the sort line and goes to landfill, where it will break down eventually and considerably better than a plastic-lined cup would, but not into compost.

So is it pointless?

No, but the benefit is not the one on the sign. What you actually get is a cup with no polyethylene and no PFAS in it, made from FSC-certified board, that doesn’t shed plastic into the environment whichever waste stream it ends up in. That is worth paying for. “Turns into soil next month” mostly isn’t what happens.

How to word your signage

Two things we’d suggest. First, check your own municipality’s accepted-items list before printing anything, and check it again each year. Second, if your city doesn’t take these, say something true and specific instead — “plastic-free cups, no PFAS” is accurate, defensible, and more interesting than a green-bin claim that a well-informed customer will catch you on.
